## FAQ

### Q1: What is the main difference between Pyongyang and Hamheung naengmyeon?
Pyongyang naengmyeon originates from the mountainous north, using buckwheat for the noodles (which makes them grey and brittle) and is served in a mild, icy beef and radish broth [cite: 16, 25]. Hamheung naengmyeon comes from the coastal northeast, uses potato or sweet potato starch (making the noodles incredibly chewy), and is usually served without broth, mixed instead in a fiery red pepper sauce with raw fish [cite: 19, 25].

### Q2: Why is naengmyeon so expensive in Seoul right now?
Welcome to "Noodleflation." Between 2024 and 2026, the cost of ingredients like buckwheat and domestic beef soared, while the Korean won weakened [cite: 3, 26]. Add in rising electricity costs for running restaurant air conditioners and increased labor costs, and a bowl that used to cost ₩10,000 is now pushing ₩16,000 to ₩19,000 at famous spots [cite: 2, 14]. 

### Q3: Do I really need scissors to eat Korean cold noodles?
It entirely depends on the style! If you are eating Hamheung style (potato starch), yes, you absolutely need scissors because the noodles are so chewy and elastic they are virtually impossible to bite cleanly through [cite: 19]. If you are eating Pyongyang style (buckwheat), skip the scissors; the noodles are delicate and break easily [cite: 21]. 

### Q4: Is it weird to eat icy cold soup in the winter?
Not at all! In fact, historically, naengmyeon was a winter dish in North Korea [cite: 3]. Long before refrigeration, the only way to get the icy dongchimi (radish water kimchi) broth was to bury clay pots in the freezing winter snow. Many food purists still insist that naengmyeon tastes best in January!

### Q5: Can I eat naengmyeon if I am a vegetarian?
It's tough. Even the spicy Bibim-naengmyeon usually contains hidden beef broth in the sauce, or comes topped with raw skate fish (hoe) or sliced beef [cite: 13, 19]. Your best bet is to look for specialized vegan Korean restaurants, or stick to the meal kits at convenience stores where you can strictly control the ingredients.

### The Background Story: A Tale of Two Cities and One Brutal War

To really understand what you are eating, you have to realize that naengmyeon isn't just a bowl of soup; it is a bowl of living history. How did this dish, which is synonymous with surviving the sweltering South Korean summer, actually originate as a *winter* food in North Korea? 

Let's rewind the clock. Before refrigeration was invented, the only way to get the icy *dongchimi* (radish water kimchi) broth that forms the base of Pyongyang naengmyeon was to bury massive clay pots deep in the freezing winter snow [cite: 3]. People in the northern capital of Pyongyang would sit in their heated *ondol* rooms in the dead of winter, slurping freezing cold buckwheat noodles [cite: 28]. Why buckwheat? Because Pyongyang is surrounded by rugged, mountainous terrain where standard wheat wouldn't grow, but hardy, bitter buckwheat thrived [cite: 25]. 

Meanwhile, over on the northeastern coast of the peninsula in the city of Hamheung, the climate and geography were totally different. Hamheung was rich in potatoes and sweet potatoes, and because it was a coastal city, they had abundant access to fresh seafood [cite: 25]. So, their version of the dish evolved using potato starch for the noodles (giving them that legendary, rubber-band chewiness) and topping them with raw skate fish (*hongeohweh*) caught off the coast [cite: 19, 25]. 

So how did these two strictly North Korean regional specialties conquer the streets of modern Seoul? War. During the brutal Korean War in the early 1950s, millions of refugees fled south from Pyongyang and Hamheung to escape the fighting [cite: 9, 29]. When they settled in Seoul, they missed the taste of home. In 1953, a group of refugees opened Ojangdong Heungnamjip in central Seoul, recreating the fiery, chewy Hamheung noodles they grew up with [cite: 9, 10]. Even earlier, in 1946, a man named Jang Won-il opened Woo Lae Oak (which poetically translates to "House of Return") to serve the delicate Pyongyang style [cite: 7, 8]. 

Over the decades, these modest refugee noodle shops transformed into massive, multi-story culinary empires. In 2018, Pyongyang naengmyeon hit the global stratosphere when it was served at the historic Inter-Korean summit to Kim Jong Un, causing lines outside Seoul noodle shops to stretch for literal blocks [cite: 11]. Today, when you sit down at these restaurants, you are eating a recipe born of displacement, perfected by generations of grandmothers, and fiercely debated by foodies across the nation.

Because a standard bowl of noodles is ₩13,000 and a serving of pork belly is over ₩21,000, Korean families are doing the math and realizing that going to an all-you-can-eat buffet is actually a better deal [cite: 1]. Places like Makino Chaya or Ashley Queens are seeing explosive growth [cite: 1]. For about ₩27,000 on a weekday lunch, you can eat unlimited sushi, steak, and yes, unlimited DIY bowls of cold naengmyeon [cite: 1]. If you have a massive appetite, skip the specialty noodle shops and hit a buffet to stretch your won. 

Lastly, a warning about the exchange rate. The "noodleflation" isn't just because restaurant owners are greedy; South Korea imports 98% of its wheat and heavily relies on imported ingredients [cite: 26]. Between 2024 and 2026, the Korean Won weakened significantly against the US Dollar [cite: 26, 34]. This means that while the price in Won looks terrifyingly high to locals, if you are traveling with US Dollars or Euros, the conversion rate actually softens the blow for you [cite: 26]. Still, keep an eye on your budget, because a few bowls of premium Woo Lae Oak will add up faster than you think!
