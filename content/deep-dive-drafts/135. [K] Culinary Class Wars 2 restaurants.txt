Every single chef from the show manages their restaurant differently. If you miss the initial drop (which, let's be real, happens to the best of us), don't panic. Go to the restaurant's page on the app and turn on the "Vacancy Notification Request." When someone cancels, the app sends an alert to everyone on the waitlist. 
> **Pro Tip:** When that notification pops up on your screen, do not think, do not hesitate, just click! It's a fastest-finger-first situation, and you have about three seconds before someone else snags it.

### Step 4: Master the Same-Day Virtual Queue
For casual Black Spoon spots like Okdongsik, they don't do advance reservations. Instead, you have to show up in person and enter your details into a tablet at the door to join a virtual queue. On weekends, the machine turns on at 10:30 AM [cite: 9]. Get there early, punch in your details, and then go hang out in a nearby cafe until the app tells you it's your turn.
> **Pro Tip:** If you don't have a Korean phone number to enter into the tablet at the door, look for a QR code on the machine to scan with your phone, or politely ask a staff member to help you register using an email address or the Global app integration.

## FAQ

### Q1: Who actually won Culinary Class Wars Season 2 and where can I eat their food?
The winner of Season 2 was White Spoon chef Choi Kang-rok, who took the crown with an incredibly emotional sesame tofu dish [cite: 4, 13]. However, here's the catch—you can't eat his food right now! He permanently closed his Japanese restaurant, Neo, in early 2025 because he felt he couldn't handle the overwhelming surge in popularity, and he has explicitly stated he isn't opening a new place anytime soon [cite: 3, 4]. 

### Q2: Are the Black Spoon restaurants actually affordable for normal travelers?
Absolutely! While the White Spoon fine-dining spots can run you hundreds of dollars, many Black Spoon chefs run highly accessible eateries. For example, a bowl of the legendary pork bone soup at Okdongsik costs around ₩10,000 to ₩14,000 (roughly $8 to $11 USD) [cite: 9]. It's some of the best comfort food in the city, but you'll pay with your time waiting in line instead of your wallet.

### Q3: What's the deal with "Brewmaster Yun" and her pub Yunjudang?
Yun Na-ra, aka Brewmaster Yun, was the breakout star of the Black Spoons, impressing the notoriously strict Judge Ahn Sung-jae with her house-brewed makgeolli (rice wine) [cite: 20]. She runs a tiny, 14-seat pub in Haebangchon called Yunjudang [cite: 19, 21]. It is notoriously difficult to book—they only open reservations on the 15th of the month, and they have a strict rule that every table must order at least one bottle of traditional liquor [cite: 19]. 

### Q4: I can't get a reservation anywhere. Is there a backup plan?
If you can't score a booking at the flagship restaurants, look for their casual offshoots or outside-of-Seoul locations! For example, Chef Jung Ho-young's main Izakaya Caden in Seoul might be packed, but he also operates a massive Udon Caden location on Jeju Island [cite: 16]. Alternatively, if you're traveling back to the US, runner-up Culinary Monster (Lee Ha-sung) is opening his highly anticipated restaurant *Oyatte* in New York City in the spring of 2026 [cite: 14, 15].

### Q5: How much do these fine-dining White Spoon meals cost?
If you're aiming for a Michelin-starred experience like Chef Son Jong-won's Eatanic Garden or L'Amant Secret, be prepared to splurge. A lunch course typically starts around ₩150,000 to ₩240,000 ($115 - $180), while dinner tasting menus soar to ₩270,000 to ₩380,000 ($200 - $285) per person [cite: 8]. You're paying for flawless technique, premium domestic ingredients (like Hanwoo beef), and a view of the Seoul skyline from the 36th floor [cite: 22].

If you're a digital nomad or a budget traveler looking for soul-warming food that won't break the bank, you want the **Black Spoon Casual** spots. You absolutely must hit up **Okdongsik** in Mapo-gu. Chef Ok Dong-sik makes a clear pork bone soup (Dwaeji Gomtang) that is so good, his New York branch is perpetually overbooked [cite: 20]. For less than 15 bucks, you get a bowl of perfectly clear, flavorful broth with paper-thin slices of Jiri Mountain Berkshire pork [cite: 9, 27]. Similarly, if you want massive portions, head to Chef Jung Ho-young's **Udon Caden** in Seodaemun-gu. It's a casual, bustling noodle shop where a bowl of perfectly chewy, thick udon costs about ₩10,000, and they'll give you up to three free noodle refills if you're starving [cite: 16, 30, 31]! 

If you're traveling with friends and want to experience Seoul's incredible nightlife, target the **Hidden Gem Pubs**. The absolute must-visit is **Yunjudang**, Brewmaster Yun's tiny 14-seat makgeolli house hidden on a steep hill in Haebangchon [cite: 18, 19, 21]. It is cozy, intimate, and feels like drinking in your coolest friend's living room. Just know that it's highly curated—you have to order traditional liquor, and they don't allow walk-ins [cite: 19].

### Section 5: The Stuff Nobody Tells You

Here's where I save you from making the classic tourist mistakes that I learned the hard way. First, let's talk about the elephant in the room: **Chef Choi Kang-rok**. He won the whole damn show [cite: 4, 13], and naturally, everyone wants to eat his food. Here's the brutal truth: you can't. He closed his incredibly popular Japanese restaurant, Neo, way back in January 2025 [cite: 2]. He stated on record that he was overwhelmed and has zero plans to open a new spot right now, opting instead to save his prize money to maybe open a humble noodle shop when he's much older [cite: 3, 33]. If you see some random pop-up in Myeongdong claiming to serve his famous sesame tofu, walk away. It's a tourist trap.

Instead, pivot to the runner-up, Culinary Monster (Lee Ha-sung). If you're an American tourist, you don't even need to fly to Seoul to eat his food! He is opening his highly anticipated contemporary restaurant, **Oyatte**, in New York City in the spring of 2026 [cite: 14, 15, 20]. 

Now, let's talk about the **No-ARC/No-Phone Number dilemma**. This is the single biggest point of friction for tourists in Korea. Nearly every automated kiosk in the country requires a Korean phone number (starting with 010) to send you a text message when your table is ready. If you just bought a cheap data-only eSIM, you don't have a number. If you walk up to a restaurant tablet and punch in your American or British phone number, the machine will flat-out reject it. 

**Here is the hack:** Look at the tablet carefully. CatchTable recently updated most of their hardware to display a small QR code in the corner specifically for foreigners. You open the CatchTable Global app on your phone, hit the scan button, scan the QR code, and it syncs your place in line via push notifications over Wi-Fi/Data instead of SMS text messages. If the tablet is an older model without a QR code, you must swallow your pride, walk inside, and politely say, *"Jeo-neun oe-guk-in in-de, jeon-hwa-beon-ho ga eop-seo-yo"* (I am a foreigner and don't have a phone number). The staff are used to this! They will pull out a master tablet, input a dummy number for you, and physically write down your queue number on a piece of paper, telling you what time to return. 

Lastly, understand the culture of **"Break Time."** Almost every single restaurant mentioned on the show—from high-end Haobin to casual Okdongsik—completely shuts down between 3:00 PM and 5:00 PM (or 6:00 PM) [cite: 21, 28]. The staff are eating their own meals and prepping for dinner. You cannot walk in at 3:30 PM expecting a late lunch. Plan your eating schedule meticulously around these break times, or you'll find yourself eating convenience store kimbap while staring longingly through a locked glass door.
