## Listen for the end of the toast

**건배** (*geonbae*) means “a toast” and works as “Cheers!” when people raise their glasses. The [National Institute of Korean Language’s dictionary entry](https://krdict.korean.go.kr/kor/dicSearch/SearchView?ParaWordNo=23629) has both meanings and an audio button for the word.

If the host or another guest starts speaking, wait to see whether they’re proposing a toast. A lifted glass and the final **건배** are cues to join. When people across a wide table lift their glasses, you can raise yours where you’re seated rather than reaching over dishes to touch every glass. If you miss the first cue, you can raise your glass with the others when you notice.

## Use one short sentence if you’re asked to lead

At a meal, give people a moment to hear you. Pick a sentence that fits the occasion and finish with **건배**. You can say the first sentence in English and still end with the Korean word. If the event has an emcee or a planned order of speeches, check when they want you to speak.

| Occasion | A short toast |
| --- | --- |
| Thanking the host | **초대해 주셔서 감사합니다. 건배!** Thank you for inviting me. Cheers! |
| Celebrating a birthday | **생일 축하합니다. 건배!** Happy birthday. Cheers! |
| Thanking people at a reunion | **다시 만나서 반갑습니다. 건배!** It’s good to see you again. Cheers! |

The [dictionary gives the host thank-you in a meal setting](https://krdict.korean.go.kr/eng/dicSearch/SearchView?ParaWordNo=76439&nation=eng&nationCode=6). Use the birthday or reunion line only when it describes the people at your table. If you’re unsure of someone’s name or title, it’s enough to thank the whole group for coming together.

If the table wants to play after the toast, [learn two games that work without drinks](/blog/korean-drinking-games-rules-and-fun).

## Keep the toast separate from what’s in the glass

You can join with the drink you already have, including water, or just smile and listen. When leading, invite people to raise their glasses and let each guest decide whether to sip.
